About

I’m an engineer on NVIDIA’s Power Architecture Analysis team. The team analyses the power architecture of our chips, and I write the software it does that with.

Most of my days go into internal tools: a Rust back end, a React front end, and Python for everything else. Off the clock: competitive programmer, gamer, binge-watcher, collector of small trinkets from new places, and occasionally Batman.

The team’s power engineers run analyses on this platform all day, and each run took 1 min 45 s. I rebuilt it with a Rust back end and a React front end.

PACE: the team’s memory, searchable

What the team knew lived in code and Confluence pages, scattered and sometimes out of date. PACE (Power Architecture Chat Engine) is a retrieval-augmented chat engine over both, built on OpenWebUI, ChromaDB, LiteLLM and FastAPI.

Twelve interns ramped up with it and rated it 9.5 out of 10. Its wrong answers turned out to be useful too: they pointed at stale documents, so we refreshed them. I designed it as a base for agentic workflows, and it won NTech 2025, NVIDIA’s internal paper presentation competition.

Python · FastAPI · ChromaDB · LiteLLM · OpenWebUI

Springer · 2026 · final-year project

Teaching StarCraft II to think in tactics

Most reinforcement-learning agents learn StarCraft II one click at a time. Ours chose between higher-level tactics instead: a tactic manager, built on a knowledge-infused policy gradient with upper-confidence-bound exploration, picked a tactic from the game state and the rewards it had seen.

We tested it against DeepMind’s baseline agent, a Grandmaster and a novice across five mini-games. It beat the baseline at gathering resources and building units, and struggled in fights, where adapting fast matters more than having a plan.

Varshney, A., Seshadri, K., Andraju, V. K. “Assessment of the Knowledge-Infused Policy Gradient Approach for StarCraft.” ICT for Intelligent Systems (ICTIS 2025), Springer, 2026, pp. 113–123. doi:10.1007/978-981-95-1357-4_9
